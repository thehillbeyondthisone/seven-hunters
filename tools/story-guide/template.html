The premise

You inherit a duty,
and an absence.

It is 3 January 1901. The relief boat puts Walter Innes ashore on Eilean Mòr, but the swell drives it away before the other keepers can land. He is alone in the station where three men vanished in December.

The immediate task is simple: exhibit the light from sunset to sunrise. Beyond that are the empty rooms, the sea, and Ceit Macleod, an observer on Gallan Head who watches your light each night.

The design’s central tension: what you do, what you think you saw, and what you are willing to put into the official record.

What is established?

The historical disappearance and contemporary reports anchor the setting. Walter, Ceit, the Board’s instructional letter and their conversations are fiction.

The current demo contains one night and the following morning. Later acts, photographs, extra duties and consequences across days belong to the plan, not the playable game.

New rooms and furnishings are period-plausible reconstructions. They should not turn an invented clue into a claim about the real missing men.

Walter Innes

The keeper

An assistant keeper from Leith. His past is largely open. We can shape him through what he tells Ceit, what he notices and what he omits.

Ceit Macleod

The distant voice

Dry humour, loneliness and guilt: she watched in December, but told herself the missing light was hidden by weather. Contact depends on clear air.

The absent three

The empty station

Ducat, Marshall and MacArthur remain present through the record and their absence. The station’s ordinary work should make that absence felt.

The long-form plan · proposed

The Keeping

Learn the station and its routines. Explore the island. Build trust with Ceit and a clear memory of how things normally look.

The Haar

Contact comes and goes. Small discrepancies accumulate: a gate, a light, an entry. Rumour competes with the record.

The Extra Large Sea

A great gale takes the story toward the west landing, followed by the relief. The exact climax and resolution remain writing decisions.

Optional epilogue in the plan: develop the keeper’s photographs on Lewis. Photography and this epilogue are not built.

Proposed · duration and rhythm are open

How long should the watch last?

The master plan spans roughly 35–40 calendar days. We can keep that sense of isolation while choosing how many days the player actually plays.

Discussion starting point

Selected watches

Play a small set of consequential days across several weeks. Use dated transitions for ordinary days.

Gain: space for the relationship, with less repetition.
Tradeoff: transitions must make accumulated time and trust believable.

Alternative

A compact stay

Tell the complete arc over a few connected watches, with each session changing something important.

Gain: urgency, a clear shape, a smaller writing scope.
Tradeoff: loneliness and closeness have less time to develop.

Original broad scope

The long posting

Play many daily routines over the planned posting, punctuated by conversations, weather and discoveries.

Gain: a strong sense of living and working there.
Tradeoff: much more writing and activity variety; repetition needs careful control.

Decisions we can make independently

  • How much routine happens in real time, and when the player can skip ahead.
  • Whether contact windows are generous or create pressure.
  • Whether a missed exchange returns later, changes the conversation, or remains an absence.
  • How soon the first discrepancy appears, and how often another follows.

The first night’s clock today

Before the lamp is lit: one game minute per 2.5 real seconds. After lighting: one game minute per real second. The clock slows to a quarter of that rate while Ceit sends.

Reading pages and choosing replies pause the clock. Sending a reply adds its listed time cost. “Keep the watch” skips to the next event or morning.
